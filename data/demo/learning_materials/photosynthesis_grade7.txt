Grade 7 Science: Photosynthesis and Plant Nutrition
Synthetic demonstration learning material

Plants make glucose during photosynthesis. In the leaves, chlorophyll captures energy from sunlight. The light is an energy source for the process; it is not matter that becomes the plant's food. The plant stores some of the captured energy in glucose, a sugar that can be used for growth and other life processes.

Photosynthesis uses carbon dioxide from the air and water from the soil. Carbon dioxide enters leaves through tiny openings called stomata. Water travels from the roots through the stem to the leaves. Using light energy, plant cells rearrange carbon dioxide and water to make glucose and release oxygen. A simplified equation is: carbon dioxide + water + light energy → glucose + oxygen.

Roots absorb water and dissolved minerals from the soil. Minerals help plants grow, but soil is not the source of the glucose plants use as food. Most of a plant's dry mass comes from carbon atoms that entered as carbon dioxide and were built into sugars during photosynthesis. Water is also a required ingredient in the process.

Keep the roles separate when describing photosynthesis: sunlight supplies energy, carbon dioxide and water supply matter, glucose is produced, and oxygen is released. Plants also use cellular respiration to release energy from glucose. Respiration and photosynthesis are different processes and should not be described as simply reversing one another in every part of a plant.

When checking a plant's needs, remember that healthy growth depends on light, water, carbon dioxide, suitable temperature, and minerals. A plant may grow poorly if it lacks water or minerals, but this does not mean that it takes its food directly from soil. The plant produces glucose in its cells and transports sugars to parts that need them.
